# A Fair and Transparent Electoral Information Marketplace

**Version 0.10 — 13 November 2026**

**Purpose:** To strengthen New Zealand's democracy by ensuring that political ideas
can compete on their merits, that voters have equitable access to verifiable
information, and that the electoral information environment is transparent, fair,
and resistant to hidden influence — while protecting freedom of expression and
avoiding excessive state control.

---

## 1. Principles

- **Freedom of expression:** Political speech, including independent promotion of
  parties and candidates, is lawful and protected.
- **Transparency over enforced neutrality:** The state should not attempt to determine
  "truth" or "bias" in political debate. It should ensure procedural fairness,
  standardised formatting, and public accountability.
- **Equitable access:** All registered parties and candidates should have a reasonable
  opportunity to communicate their core messages, and voters should have reasonable
  access to information through a range of media.
- **Distributed oversight:** No single body should have untrammelled control over
  election information.
- **Proportional enforcement:** Penalties should match the seriousness of breaches,
  with disqualification reserved only for the most serious deliberate misconduct and
  only after independent judicial process.

---

## 2. Public Election Information Portal

The Electoral Commission will establish and administer a public election information
portal.

- **Standardised format, not edited content:** Parties and candidates will submit their
  policy statements using a common template. The Commission will publish the statements
  in that standardised format but will **not** edit, summarise, or adjudicate the
  content for "bias" or "truth."
- **Raw policy statements:** The portal will contain the full policy statements of
  registered parties and candidates, plus any additional material they wish to provide,
  clearly labelled as party-supplied material.
- **Independent procedural audit:** An independent audit body will review the
  Commission's processes for procedural fairness, accessibility, and consistency. It
  will **not** adjudicate the truth of political claims.
- **Multi-media accessibility:** Information will be made available through the portal,
  public broadcasting, accessible formats, and support for public-interest journalism.
- **Monitoring and reporting:** The Commission will monitor and report after each
  election on how effectively the information was disseminated, including reach,
  accessibility, and public awareness.

---

## 3. Multi-Party Oversight Board

A multi-party oversight board will be established to oversee the portal and related
rules.

- Membership will include representatives from all registered parties, civil society,
  academia, and independent electoral experts.
- The board will approve the operational rules for the portal, review audit findings,
  and publish an annual accountability report.
- The Commission will administer the portal, but the board will provide oversight and
  ensure no single body controls the information environment.
- The Commission's performance will be subject to independent external review after
  each election.

---

## 4. Equitable Access to Information

- All registered parties and candidates will have a reasonable opportunity to
  communicate their core messages to the public.
- This will be supported through:
  - the public election information portal;
  - the existing broadcasting allocation, reviewed to ensure fair and reasonable access;
  - support for public-interest journalism and civic information;
  - accessible formats for people with disabilities and diverse communities.
- Success will be measured by whether voters have reasonable access to information and
  whether parties and candidates have a reasonable opportunity to communicate their
  messages.

---

## 5. Regulation of Independent Promotion

Independent political speech is lawful and protected. It will be regulated through a
strengthened third-party regime.

- **Lower registration thresholds** for third parties engaging in regulated election
  advertising (for example, from $17,000 to $5,000).
- **Stricter spending caps** for third-party campaigners.
- **Real-time disclosure** of large donations and significant spending by third-party
  promoters.
- **Clear labelling** requirements for election advertising, including on social media.
- **Verification and enforcement** by the Electoral Commission, with appeals to an
  independent body.

This addresses hidden or "shadow" campaigning without criminalising core political
expression.

---

## 6. Money in Politics

### 6.1 Donation cap

A strict limit on how much any single donor can give to a party and its candidates.

- **The cap:** $30,000 per donor, per electoral cycle, across a party and all of its
  candidates.
- **Who can donate:** Only individuals enrolled to vote may donate. Companies and
  organisations are banned from donating.

### 6.2 Expenditure-side equity

- **The measure:** Substantially lower the regulated spending limit for parties and
  candidates, so that a party funded by broad-based, capped donations can realistically
  reach the ceiling. The exact figure to be set through consultation.

### 6.3 Electoral vouchers

- **The mechanism:** Every enrolled voter allocated a voucher worth approximately $20
  per electoral cycle, which they may direct to the registered party of their choice.
- **The effect:** Public funding is channelled through citizens rather than through
  party hierarchies or wealthy donors, giving every voter the same financial voice.
- **Funding:** Met by redirecting existing public funding and the revenue foregone from
  capping large donations, rather than being purely new expenditure.

### 6.4 Real-time disclosure

The threshold for immediate disclosure of donations during an election year should be
lowered (for example, to $5,000), so that large donations are visible to voters before
they cast their ballots.

## 7. Protection of Electoral Deliberation

### 7.1 Purpose

In casting a vote, a person is discharging a public function on behalf of the electoral
system. The integrity of that function is a matter of public interest, and certain forms
of interference with it are properly treated as public wrongs. This clause protects the
integrity of the voter's decision from fraudulent and deceptive interference, without
restricting the political persuasion on which democratic choice depends.

### 7.2 The jury analogy

A juror, once empanelled, becomes a temporary extension of the judicial system and is
subject to strict rules about communication and influence. Section 117(b) of the Crimes
Act 1961 criminalises influencing a juror by "threats or bribes or other corrupt means,"
with a maximum penalty of seven years' imprisonment. The rationale is that the juror is
performing a state function, and corrupting that function harms the integrity of the
system.

A voter performs an analogous function. Section 218 of the Electoral Act 1993 creates an
offence of undue influence, classified as a corrupt practice, capturing influence by
force, violence, or restraint, threats of temporal or spiritual injury, or abduction,
duress, or any fraudulent device or contrivance. The Local Electoral Act 2001 contains a
parallel provision.

This clause builds on that architecture. It does not create a general offence of
influencing voters. A juror is instructed to exclude external persuasion; a voter is
expected to be exposed to it. The offence is therefore confined to fraud and deception —
conduct that corrupts the voter's decision by defeating the very capacity to weigh
competing ideas that the electoral system exists to protect.

### 7.3 Protected electoral deliberation period

Begins on writ day and ends at the close of the polls.

### 7.4 Offence: corrupt interference with electoral deliberation

A person commits an offence who, during the protected period, engages in conduct intended
to, and likely to, corrupt or deceive a voter in the exercise of their vote, being
conduct involving:

1. **Fraudulent contrivance:** using a fraudulent device or contrivance to induce a voter
   to vote, or refrain from voting, in a particular way;
2. **Impersonation of electoral authority:** falsely representing themselves as the
   Electoral Commission, an electoral official, or a candidate or authorised
   representative, for the purpose of influencing a vote;
3. **Deceptive misrepresentation of the electoral process:** knowingly making a false or
   misleading representation about the time, place, or manner of voting, a voter's
   eligibility or enrolment, or the effect of a vote;
4. **Deceptive fabrication of a party's or candidate's communication:** knowingly creating
   or distributing material that falsely purports to be an official communication of a
   party or candidate, including through synthetic or manipulated media.

### 7.5 Exclusions

Nothing in this clause makes unlawful:

- ordinary political advocacy, including advertising, campaigning, endorsement,
  criticism, satire, parody, and robust comment on parties, candidates, policies, or the
  electoral process;
- opinion, prediction, or contested factual claim about the likely consequences of a
  vote, however forcefully expressed;
- journalism, academic commentary, and civil society communication;
- communications by a party or candidate about their own policies or record, including
  where inaccurate or contested.

The distinction is between persuasion that invites a voter to judge and deception that
removes the capacity to judge.

### 7.6 Penalties

- **Standard offence:** a fine and a requirement to publish a correction.
- **Aggravated offence:** a substantially higher fine and, on conviction on indictment, a
  term of imprisonment.
- **Disqualification:** only for a serious, deliberate, and sustained campaign of
  deception, and only on order of the High Court.

### 7.7 Safeguards

- Consistency with ss 12 and 14 of the New Zealand Bill of Rights Act 1990; any limit
  demonstrably justified under s 5, addressed expressly in the Attorney-General's s 7
  report.
- Offences read together with the Electoral Act 1993 and the Crimes Act 1961, not
  duplicating or displacing them.
- Prosecutions require the leave of the Solicitor-General.
- Independent review after each election, with public consultation.

## 8. Regulation of Electoral Advertising

### 8.1 Purpose

A tiered regime. Ordinary political advertising is published on self-certification, with
a promoter statement and audit. Advertising using a prescribed manipulative technique
requires pre-clearance. All advertising is subject to rapid post-publication
adjudication, with correction as the default remedy and takedown on order.

### 8.2 Definitions

- **electoral advertising:** any advertisement, in any medium, that may reasonably be
  regarded as encouraging or persuading voters to vote for or against a party, candidate,
  or type of candidate, published for payment or other consideration, or by a party or
  candidate.
- **promoter statement:** a statement identifying who authorised the advertising, who
  paid for it, and whether a prescribed manipulative technique is used.
- **authorisation code:** the code issued by the Commission on self-certification or
  pre-clearance.
- **protected period:** writ day to the close of the polls.

### 8.3 Prescribed manipulative techniques

A prescribed manipulative technique means any of the following, used in a manner that is
not transparent to the viewer:

1. **Subliminal stimulation:** words, images, or sounds presented below the threshold of
   conscious awareness, or at a speed or intensity that prevents conscious perception.
2. **Deceptive source attribution through design:** design elements calculated to cause a
   reasonable viewer to attribute the advertising to a different party, candidate, or
   official body than the one that authorised it.
3. **Synthetic or manipulated media presented as authentic:** material depicting a real
   person saying or doing something they did not say or do, where a reasonable viewer
   would not recognise it as synthetic.
4. **Manufactured grassroots endorsement:** fabricated "independent," "grassroots," or
   "community" endorsement, where the material is in fact paid for or controlled by a
   party, candidate, or third-party campaigner.

Additional techniques may be prescribed by regulation only on the advice of the
oversight board and after public consultation, and only where specifically defined and
calculable to impair conscious evaluation of the message.

### 8.4 Self-certification for ordinary advertising

1. A promoter statement must be submitted to the Commission before publication.
2. The Commission issues an authorisation code automatically on receipt of a complete
   statement, without reviewing content.
3. The code must appear on the advertising, or be reasonably accessible to the viewer.
4. Advertising with a valid code is lawfully published unless and until a remedial order
   is made.

### 8.5 Pre-clearance for prescribed techniques

1. Application at least 5 working days before intended publication.
2. Application includes full content, the promoter statement, and a description of the
   technique and its intended effect.
3. The Commission must, within 24 hours of receiving an application, notify the applicant
   whether it is complete and, if not, what is missing. An application not rejected as
   incomplete within that period is deemed complete. The Commission must decide an
   application within 48 hours of receiving it or, where the Commission has notified the
   applicant that it is incomplete, within 48 hours of receiving the corrected application.
4. The Commission may grant, grant with conditions (including modification), or refuse.
5. If no decision is made within the applicable 48-hour period under sub-clause 3, the
   application is deemed granted and a code issued immediately.
6. Refusal or conditions may be appealed to the High Court within 24 hours; the Court
   must determine the appeal within 48 hours.
7. An applicant may correct an application within 24 hours of an incompleteness notice. A
   corrected application is taken to have been received on the date of the original
   application for the purposes of sub-clause 1, but the 48-hour period runs from its
   actual receipt. The Commission may reject an application as incomplete on one occasion
   only, and the notice must be confined to the matters in sub-clause 2.
8. A code issued under sub-clause 5 is a deemed code. The Commission must record it on the
   public register and review the advertising under s 8.7 within 3 working days. Section
   8.8(2) applies to advertising published under a deemed code as if it had not been
   pre-cleared.

### 8.6 Publication of unauthorised material

1. A person commits an offence who knowingly or recklessly publishes, or causes or
   permits to be published, electoral advertising without a code, or using a prescribed
   technique without pre-clearance.
2. Media outlets, broadcasting platforms, and online platforms carrying advertising
   bearing a valid code are not liable.
3. An individual sharing advertising in a non-commercial, personal capacity is not
   liable.

### 8.7 Rapid adjudication

The Commission may act on complaint or its own initiative, and must determine a complaint
within 3 working days, extendable once by a further 3 working days with reasons. Appeals
to the High Court within 24 hours.

### 8.8 Remedial orders

1. The default remedy is a correction order, requiring publication of a correction within
   24 hours in a form and manner specified.
2. A takedown order requires removal within 24 hours, but only where the advertising uses
   a prescribed technique without pre-clearance and a correction would not adequately
   remedy the harm.
3. Takedown orders may be appealed to the High Court within 24 hours, determined within
   48 hours, with the order suspended pending determination.
4. Every correction and takedown order is published on the public register with reasons.

### 8.9 Exclusions

Nothing in this clause makes unlawful ordinary political advocacy (including robust
criticism, exaggeration, satire, and parody); transparently partisan advertising clearly
authorised and using conventional techniques; journalism, academic commentary, and civil
society communication; advertising that is primarily informational even if selective or
one-sided; or organic social media content posted by individuals in a personal capacity.

### 8.10 Penalties

- **Standard offence:** a fine and a correction order.
- **Aggravated offence:** a substantially higher fine and, on conviction on indictment, a
  term of imprisonment.
- **Disqualification:** only for a serious, deliberate, and sustained campaign, and only
  on order of the High Court.

### 8.11 Safeguards

- Consistency with ss 12 and 14 NZBORA; s 5 justification required, addressed expressly
  in the Attorney-General's s 7 report.
- The oversight board approves operational rules, reviews decisions for consistency, and
  publishes an annual accountability report.
- Prosecutions require the leave of the Solicitor-General.
- A public register of all codes, pre-clearance decisions, correction orders, and
  takedown orders, with reasons.
- Independent review after each election, with public consultation.

### 8.12 Explanatory note: the s 5 analysis

The clause is structured to meet the minimal impairment limb of the s 5 test.

- Self-certification means the Commission does not review the content of most political
  speech; the interference is limited to a procedural formality serving transparency and
  audit.
- Pre-clearance is confined to a narrow, enumerated list, extendable only by regulation
  on the advice of the oversight board and after consultation, with a threshold designed
  to exclude ordinary partisan emphasis.
- The 48-hour deemed-approval rule converts delay from a mechanism of suppression into a
  mechanism of permission; a slow or captured Commission cannot silence speech by
  inaction.
- Correction is the default remedy; takedown requires a higher threshold and is
  appealable, with suspension pending appeal.
- Media, platforms, and individuals sharing content are excluded from liability.
- The Broadcasting Act 1989 contains a limited notification regime for broadcast election
  programmes, confined to that channel. This clause builds on that precedent but confines
  pre-clearance to techniques rather than content, and extends it to paid digital
  advertising above a reach threshold prescribed by regulation.

The clause accepts that some restriction of expression is involved in pre-clearance. Its
justification rests on the proposition that the capacity for conscious, reflective
judgment is the precondition of meaningful democratic choice, and that techniques
calculated to bypass that capacity attack the integrity of the electoral process itself.

---

## 9. Proportional Enforcement

- **Minor or technical breaches:** fines, correction notices, or compliance requirements.
- **Serious breaches:** higher fines, public disclosure, or referral to an independent
  court.
- **Disqualification:** reserved only for serious, deliberate, and sustained violations,
  and only after a full hearing by an independent court, not by an administrative body.

---

## 10. Legal Safeguards and Review

- The regime will be designed to be consistent with the New Zealand Bill of Rights Act
  1990, including ss 12 and 14.
- Any limits on expression will be demonstrably justified under s 5 and subject to regular
  review.
- The entire framework will be reviewed after each election by an independent panel, with
  public consultation.

## 11. Justification and Evidence

### 11.1 The courts' recognition of the problem

In *Electoral Commission v Watson*, the Court of Appeal stated that Parliament intended to
"limit the influence of money on the electoral process" and to preserve "equality of
voice among participants." The objective of reducing financial influence is therefore not
foreign to New Zealand law; it is a purpose the courts have already accepted as
legitimate.

The question is not whether the state may act, but how far it may go. Under s 5 NZBORA a
limit must be demonstrably justified and impair the right no more than is reasonably
necessary. The measures in this proposal are designed as proportionate limits that reduce
the risk of domination by wealth without eliminating political expression.

### 11.2 Evidence that influence is compounding

- **Party donations:** In the 2023 election, the six parties in Parliament collectively
  declared almost $25 million in donations — the highest ever and roughly three times the
  2017 total. National alone raised $10.4 million, more than double Labour's total.
- **Third-party spending:** Spending by third-party lobby groups rose from approximately
  $147,000 in 2020 to nearly $2 million in 2023 — a 13-fold increase in a single electoral
  cycle, a shift toward less regulated vehicles.
- **Weaknesses acknowledged within the system:** Academic analysis describes the framework
  as containing no limits on domestic political donations, weak disclosure standards, and
  no political expenditure limits outside the election period, making the system
  vulnerable to undue influence and distortion by private wealth. In the "sham donors"
  prosecution, the Court of Appeal itself noted that the inability to charge the
  defendants under the Electoral Act exposed a significant weakness in the offence
  provisions of that Act.

### 11.3 Why these reforms are the proportionate response

A total ban on private election spending would likely be characterised as an exception to
the s 14 right rather than a limit, and would struggle to satisfy the minimal impairment
limb. The measures here are designed to fall on the limit side of that line:

- **Donation caps** reduce the capacity of any single donor to acquire access and
  influence.
- **A lower spending ceiling** compresses the advantage of the best-funded actors.
- **Electoral vouchers** build a broad-based funding alternative so that parties are not
  dependent on large donors.

Together these address the compounding of financial advantage while remaining consistent
with the courts' recognition that equality of voice among participants is a legitimate
objective and that proportionate limits on expressive spending are justified.
